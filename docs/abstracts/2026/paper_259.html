A proof of concept simulator including dynamic interactions in dense suburban railway context

Mehdi Baali, Rémi Coulaud, Christine Buisson

Conference
hEART 2026: 14th Symposium of the European Association for Research in Transportation (2026)
Publication year
2026

Abstract

8 Dense suburban railway systems are subject to various types of hazards whose effects on supply 9 and demand interact at short time scales: dynamic interactions. The combination of hazards and 10 dynamic interactions may lead delays to propagate and worsen, which significantly deteriorates the 11 system’s performance. As such, it is crucial to simulate these effects to design robust schedules. 12 However, existing simulators for dense suburban railway systems do not fully include demand-to13 supply and supply-to-demand dynamic interactions. In this paper, a proof of concept simulator is 14 presented to show the importance of considering demand-to-supply and supply-to-demand dynamic 15 interactions. Compared to a benchmark simulator that neglects these dynamic interactions, on 16 the presented case study, our simulator is able to highlight additional phenomena resulting from 17 demand-to-supply and supply-to-demand dynamic interactions. In practice, the implementation 18 of all dynamic interactions in a ready-to-use simulator will significantly improve future scheduling. 19 This work is a first step toward this goal in which a proof of concept is provided. 20 Keywords: Demand, Disruption, Public Transport, Railway, Simulation, Supply

21 1 Introduction

22 Urban sprawl in big cities has challenged existing transport modes for commuting between the 23 suburbs and the city center. People need high-performance public transport systems to travel 24 large distances twice a day. Metros, generally underground, are often confined to the city center 25 and nearby suburbs. Differing from metros, suburban trains are often used to connect more distant 26 suburbs to the city center. However, these transport systems also need to be robust. A robust 27 system is a system that has the ability to maintain or recover, by itself, a decent performance 28 after a small hazard (Goverde & Hansen, 2013; Jensen, 2015; Lusby et al., 2018). The robustness 29 of a transport system is generally assessed using simulators considering the specificities of the 30 underlying transport mode. 31 Suburban trains and metros are close transport systems: both depend on railways, and both have 32 high capacities. However, they differ in four main points (that may not be true for all networks, 33 as the separation between train and metro is rather fluid). 34 1. Train infrastructure is generally shared between many train lines and can also be shared 35 between several train types. Metro lines circulate in segregated infrastructures. 36 2. Train lines may be composed of several branches. Services may also vary: omnibus services 37 (serving all stations), semi-direct services (serving some stations but not all), and direct 38 services (serving only the termini). Metro lines have a restricted number of branches and 39 services, usually only one. 40 3. Train operations are generally schedule-based, which means that a train is not allowed to 41 depart from a station before its scheduled departure time. Metro operations are headway42 based, which means that metros leave the origin terminus with a fixed headway and run 43 without schedules. 44 4. Trains are often run with a lower frequency than metros, and with higher differences in 45 services during peak periods and off-peak periods.

46 Dense suburban railway systems have similarities to the metros (Altazin, 2018). Their infrastruc47 ture is open to other trains, but in nominal situations, they are essentially dedicated to a single 48 train line. The train line is generally made up of several branches that run between a single city 49 center and several parts of the suburbs. The demand at the branches is generally lower, so the 50 frequency is less, and this justifies the operation to be schedule-based to minimize the waiting time 51 spent by passengers for a given train. The demand in the central section is generally higher due 52 to higher urban densities. At the same time, trains from the different branches merge, resulting in 53 higher frequencies, frequencies generally as high as that of a metro. Figure 1 shows an archetype 54 of a dense suburban train line. 55 In such a line, fluctuations related to operations (running times or dwell times) (Yuan et al., 2010; 56 Buchmüller et al., 2008) or passenger demand (Pan et al., 2017) are numerous and dynamically 57 interact between them. This leads delays to appear more frequently and to propagate more easily 58 compared to other types of train systems. 59 Interactions between supply and demand appear at short-term scale (dynamic interactions): for 60 example, between one vehicle and the vehicle directly after, between one station and the next 61 station, or between one vehicle and its demand. Dynamic interactions are of four types: 62 • Supply-to-supply dynamic interactions, related to signaling (Zieger et al., 2018; Huang et 63 al., 2024); 64 • Demand-to-demand dynamic interactions, related to boarding failure (Leurent et al., 2014; 65 Hänseler et al., 2020; Cornet, 2020) ; 66 • Demand-to-supply dynamic interactions, related to dwell time delays that may appear when 67 the alighting and boarding volumes are too large (Cornet et al., 2019; Palmqvist et al., 2020; 68 Coulaud et al., 2023; Baali, Buisson, et al., 2025); 69 • Supply-to-demand dynamic interactions, related to passenger arrival behaviors that can be 70 split into headway-based passengers (who arrive uniformly at the station, knowing a train 71 will arrive shortly) and schedule-based passengers (aiming for a train scheduled at a specific 72 time) (Baali et al., 2026). 73 These dynamic interactions (illustrated in Figure 2) have been extensively studied in the literature. 74 However, understanding them is not sufficient to solve operational performance and robustness 75 issues. They need to be addressed through simulation tools. Existing simulators are limited to other 76 transport modes, such as buses or metros (Toledo et al., 2010; Fonzone et al., 2015; Srikukenthiran 77 & Shalaby, 2017; D’Acierno et al., 2017), or do not consider all dynamic interactions (Landex 78 & Nielsen, 2006; Cornet, 2020; Othman et al., 2023). To our knowledge, no simulator for dense 79 suburban train systems that integrates all dynamic interactions exists so far. 80 Therefore, the goal of this work is to provide a proof of concept simulator for dense suburban 81 trains. The proof of concept will help highlight several phenomena related to supply-to-demand 82 and demand-to-supply dynamic interactions that cannot be spotted without considering them. 83 The paper is structured as follows. First, a simulator integrating all dynamic interactions is pre84 sented along with a benchmark simulator. Then, a comparison is provided between both simulator 85 results for a case study in Paris. This paper ends with a conclusion.

86 2 Methodology

87 In the literature, railway simulator commonly do not consider all dynamic interactions. Supply-to88 supply dynamic interactions are integrated but demand and supply are considered separately. A 89 simplified version of such a simulator will serve as a benchmark in this work (Figure 3 right). By 90 contrast, we present a simplified simulator considering all the dynamic interactions (Figure 3 right). 91 This simplified simulator is only a proof-of-concept The comparison between both simulators will 92 serve to demonstrate the ability of our simulator to simulate phenomena that typically result from 93 supply-to-demand and demand-to-supply dynamic interactions.

Trains from other lines or 153 branches may hinder trains from the studied line due to signaling (supply-to-supply dynamic 154 interactions), but we will neglect them in this work. 155 The above-presented equations are cyclic because they depend on each other. As a reminder, 156 Figure 3 illustrates the dependencies between the variables. To solve this, the computation for any 157 (k, s, d) is done twice iteratively. First, the demand-to-supply dynamic interaction is neglected (the 158 alighting and boarding time is assumed to be zero). Then, the observed departure time, headway, 159 and number of boardings are computed from there. Based on the computed number of boardings, 160 the observed departure time is computed again with the demand-to-supply dynamic interaction 161 (influence of the alighting and boarding time).
